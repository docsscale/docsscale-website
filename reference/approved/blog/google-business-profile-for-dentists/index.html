Google Business Profile for dentists: what to fix first

Your Google Business Profile is what most new patients see before your website. Here is what to fix first, in the order that matters, and what you can safely ignore.

  • Reviews and how you answer them do more for a dental profile than any trick. Never confirm in a reply that someone is your patient.
  • Check the profile once a month. Most of the work is ten minutes of upkeep, not a one-time project.

When someone searches "dentist near me", the first thing they see is not your website. It is a map with three dental practices under it. Whether yours is one of them, and whether the person taps it, depends mostly on your Google Business Profile.

A Google Business Profile for dentists works the same way as for any local business, with two differences: reviews carry more weight, because people are choosing someone to trust with their teeth, and patient privacy limits what you can say in public. This guide covers both.

Most dental profiles we look at are not bad. They are half-finished: set up once, years ago, and never opened again. The fixes that matter are simple, and you can do the first ones today.

Set up the four things patients act on

Before photos, posts or anything clever, check these four. They are what a person actually uses.

  1. Your name. Use the practice's real name, the one on your door. Do not add words like "best dentist" or the city. Google can suspend a profile for that, and getting it back takes weeks.
  2. Your hours. Wrong hours cost you patients quietly: people arrive at a closed door or do not call because Google says you are shut. Add your holiday hours too.
  3. One phone number. The number that is answered by a person during opening hours. If calls go to voicemail at lunch, that is worth fixing before anything on this list.
  4. A booking link. Google lets you add an appointment link. Point it at the page where a new patient can really ask for a time, not at your homepage.

Choose the right Google Business Profile category for a dental practice

Your primary category tells Google what you are. For a general practice it should be Dentist. This sounds obvious, but we often find it set to something narrower or broader.

Then add secondary categories only for what you truly do and want more of, for example cosmetic dentistry, emergency dental care or children's dentistry. Each one should match a service you offer every week. A long list of categories you rarely work in does not help.

List your dental services in patients' words

There is a services section in the profile. Fill it in, and write the way patients search: "teeth whitening", "dental implants", "emergency tooth extraction", not only the clinical terms. One clear line under each is enough.

If you want more of a particular treatment, make sure it is here, on your website, and in the photos. Google is more likely to show you for a treatment when all three agree. This is the core of local SEO for a dental practice: the profile and the website saying the same thing.

Photos: real ones, added regularly

People choose a dentist partly on whether the place looks clean, modern and friendly. Add:

  • the outside of the building, so people recognise it when they arrive
  • the reception and a treatment room
  • the team, with their agreement
  • the dentist or dentists, smiling

Use your own photos. Stock pictures are easy to spot and do the opposite of building trust. A few new photos every month or two shows Google and patients that the practice is active.

Never post a photo that shows a patient without their written permission, including "before and after" pictures.

Google reviews: what matters most for a dental practice

For a dental practice, reviews do more than anything else on the profile. Two things matter: getting them steadily, and answering them well.

Getting them. Ask at the moment a patient is happiest, usually right after a visit that went well, and make it one tap: a link by text or email that opens the review box directly. A slow, steady flow looks natural and is worth more than a burst of twenty in one week. Do not offer discounts or gifts for reviews; Google does not allow it.

Answering them. Reply to every review, good or bad, within a few days. Keep it short and human.

Here is the rule that is special to healthcare: do not confirm that the reviewer is your patient, and never mention their treatment, even if they wrote about it themselves. Patient privacy rules still apply to your reply. A safe answer to a complaint sounds like this:

Thank you for telling us. We take every concern seriously and would like to put this right. Please call the practice and ask for the manager.

It says nothing about who they are or what was done, and it moves the conversation off the public page.

Where the profile fits: one dental practice

A good profile gets you the tap. What happens next decides whether that tap becomes a patient. One of our clients, a dental practice in Dallas, shows why.

When we started, their marketing looked fine from the outside. Two hygienists were idle on Fridays, an ad agency was reporting clicks, and the website looked fine and booked nothing after 5 pm. People were finding the practice. They were not becoming appointments.

So we did not start with more visibility. We fixed what happens after someone finds the practice: one implant funnel with three screening questions, a text back on every missed call, appointment reminders, and a recall campaign to 412 patients who had not been in for a long time, alongside Meta and Google campaigns in the practice's own accounts.

Over six months that practice booked 184 new patients, and 167 of them showed up. They are hiring a third hygienist. The full case is on our results page.

Those numbers came from the whole system, not from the profile alone, and that is the point. Before you work on being found more often, call your own number at 5:30 pm and tap your own booking link. If nobody answers and the link leads to a contact form, fix that first. Otherwise a better profile only sends more people to the same closed door.

A ten-minute monthly check

Once the profile is in order, keeping it that way is small work. Once a month:

  • confirm the hours, including any coming holidays
  • answer any reviews you have not answered
  • add two or three new photos
  • look at the profile on your own phone as a patient would, and tap the phone and booking buttons to make sure they work

Google also lets other people suggest edits to your profile, and sometimes applies them. The monthly look is how you catch a wrong phone number before your patients do.

What you can ignore

You do not need to post an update every day. You do not need to stuff keywords into your description; write two or three honest sentences about who you treat and what makes the practice easy to visit. And you do not need a separate profile for every treatment.

Get the basics right, keep reviews coming and answered, and check it monthly. That is most of the result.

Common questions

Is it worth having a Google Business Profile for a dental practice?

Yes. It is free, and it is the listing people see in Google Maps and in the map results when they search for a dentist nearby. Many of them call or ask for directions from the profile without ever opening your website.

Should each dentist in the practice have their own profile?

Usually no. One complete, well-kept profile for the practice is easier to manage and collects all your reviews in one place. Google sometimes creates separate listings for individual dentists by itself; if those split your reviews or show old details, they are worth cleaning up.

How long do changes take to show?

Small edits often appear within minutes, but Google reviews some changes first, especially to the name, address and category, and that can take several days. Plan changes ahead of a move or new opening hours.
